Every staff account has exactly one role, and that role decides everything
they can see and do. This page explains how roles work and how to build
your own safely. For the actual list of permissions to check off while
you're configuring one, see the [Permissions reference](/reference/permissions/).

## Roles are dynamic, not fixed

A role is just a name attached to a set of permissions. RedScribe ships
with four built-in roles that cover common team setups, but none of them
are locked in place:

- You can edit the permission set on any built-in role except Superadmin.
- You can create entirely new roles from scratch, with whatever
  combination of permissions actually matches how your team works.
- Managing roles needs its own permission, so it's something you grant
  deliberately, not something every admin screen assumes.

Think of the built-in roles as sensible starting points, not a hierarchy
you're locked into.

## The built-in roles

| Role | What it's built for |
|---|---|
| **Superadmin** | Full control over everything, always. Not editable, and always requires MFA no matter what your instance-wide MFA setting says. |
| **Team Lead** | Running engagements day to day: creating them, managing membership and scope, releasing findings to clients, reviewing and QA'ing findings, and managing the catalogue, checklists, and client accounts. |
| **Senior** | Reviewing and QA'ing findings, and approving catalogue entries, without the broader engagement and client management a Team Lead has. |
| **Consultant** | No standing permissions by default. A consultant's day to day access comes from being added to specific engagements, not from the role itself. |

The exact permissions behind each of these are listed in the
[Permissions reference](/reference/permissions/), grouped by role so you
can see at a glance what each one starts with.

## Every permission

Permissions are grouped into categories, roughly matching the areas of the
app they govern: engagements, the vulnerability catalogue, checklists,
reports, finding review, client portal access, and instance
administration. A role's access is just the sum of whichever permissions
it holds, nothing more.

Two of these deserve a closer look before you hand them out, because
they're broader than they might sound.

### Reviewing your own work vs. covering for someone else

Two related but different needs come up around finding review and QA:

- Letting someone review or QA a finding **they wrote themselves**. This
  matters most on a small team, where requiring a second, completely
  separate person for every single finding just isn't practical.
- Letting someone act on **any** finding's review or QA decision,
  regardless of who it's actually assigned to. This is meant for a Team
  Lead covering for a reviewer who's out sick or on leave, not for
  everyday use, since it bypasses the assignment entirely.

These are separate grants, and holding one doesn't imply the other. Give
each to whoever actually has that specific problem, and see the
[Permissions reference](/reference/permissions/) for their exact names.

### The two permissions that are Superadmin in disguise

<Aside type="danger" title="Managing users or managing roles is effectively Superadmin access">
Someone who can manage user accounts can change their own role to anything
that can manage roles. Someone who can manage roles can grant themselves,
or anyone else, every permission that exists. There's no technical wall
between "can manage users or roles" and "is Superadmin," only how far
someone chooses to take it.

**Only hand either of these to someone you'd already trust with full
Superadmin access.** Check the [Permissions reference](/reference/permissions/)
for which two permissions this applies to before assigning them.
</Aside>

## Requiring MFA per role

Beyond the instance-wide "require MFA" setting (see
[Feature flags](/admin/feature-flags/)), you can also require multi-factor
authentication on a specific role directly. That's useful if you want MFA
mandatory for, say, anyone with Team Lead access, even on an instance
where it isn't required for everyone. Superadmin requires it unconditionally
either way, and that one can't be turned off.

## Building a custom role

Create a new role from the roles screen, give it a name, and pick whichever
permissions it needs from the same categorized list. There's no restriction
on the combination, so it's worth sanity checking a new role against what
it's actually meant to do before handing it to someone, especially anything
touching user management, roles, or finding review across the whole
instance.
